Housing and social mobility in Singapore · Policy brief

Policy in the real world

Imagine two families earning the same amount over a year. One can count on a steady monthly salary and has parents who can help with renovation costs. The other gets fewer shifts some months and needs its savings for emergencies. Which family can comfortably commit to a mortgage? Looking at annual income alone, you’d miss why their answers might differ.

That question is what drew me to housing policy. I wanted to understand what happens after a family qualifies for a flat. Does buying it give them more room to plan for the future? What happens to those plans if someone loses work or takes time off to care for a parent?

What I looked at

I wrote an 11-page brief drawing on Singapore housing, income and CPF data. I looked at how irregular earnings affect the decision to borrow, how wage-linked CPF contributions shape the savings available for housing, and what changes when a family can call on relatives for help. I also put together a timeline of housing policy and affordability developments from 2015 to 2025.

The trade-off I kept coming back to

The same resale price appears in two families’ calculations. For someone approaching retirement, the value of their flat may be part of how they plan to support themselves. For a first-time buyer, that figure is the price of getting in. If I were evaluating a housing measure, I’d want to know whose position improves, at what stage of life, and over what period of time.

Why timing matters

A grant can bring the purchase price within reach. A household still has to decide whether it can take on years of payments. In the brief I followed that decision through three points in time: saving for the purchase, managing a disruption once repayments begin, and deciding later whether the flat can help fund retirement. A smaller flat or a later purchase can be a sensible way to keep cash available for caregiving or a stretch with fewer shifts.

Family support changes that calculation too. Someone whose parents can cover renovations may keep a cash buffer after moving in. Someone paying the same price out of their own savings may have much less left for an emergency. That difference is easy to miss if the measure of success is simply whether both households bought a home.

What the evidence lets me say

I used the national figures to put the policy question in context. The resale price index describes movements in the market, while aggregate CPF balances give a sense of the scale of savings. Neither tells me how much a particular first-time buyer has available when their hours are cut. The two-family example illustrates the mechanism I’m interested in. To establish how often it shapes a purchase decision, I’d need household-level evidence.

What I’d investigate next

I’d like to speak with households whose earnings vary month to month. I’d ask them to walk me through the point when they decided to buy, wait, or choose a smaller flat. National figures give me the wider picture. Hearing what tipped an actual decision would help me test the assumptions I made in the brief.
