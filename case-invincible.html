Cross-Jurisdictional Privacy Compliance Analyzer

How can identical words produce different legal outcomes?

This collaborative project compares how privacy policies are assessed under the EU GDPR and Singapore PDPA. It moves beyond binary gap detection to surface areas requiring legal judgment: including consent, purpose limitation, retention and cross-border transfers.

How we got here

Edoardo and I first connected over public policy and how institutions put rules into practice. I was interested in comparing Singapore and Europe. Privacy notices gave us a concrete place to start. We could read the same sentence, bring our respective perspectives to it, and work out what each of us would still need to ask.

Version 1 · Finding the clause

Our first prototype ran 19 keyword-based checks across five areas: consent, purpose limitation, retention, overseas transfers and third-party processing. I contributed Singapore-specific terms, mapped the PDPA provisions and worked on the comparison labels. Edoardo handled the technical implementation and the GDPR side. This gave us a repeatable first pass through a notice and a practical question for the next version: what should the reviewer do with a match?

Version 2 · Reading what the clause actually says

I designed five PDPA ambiguity flags. Each needed a trigger, an explanation of why the wording could be ambiguous, and a concrete follow-up for the person reviewing it. For example, a policy might mention retention and still leave the reader wondering what determines when a company deletes the data. We wanted to distinguish a missing clause from one that exists but leaves a substantive question open.

What I tested

In June I tested the keyword/offline mode against eight Singapore-facing policies and addenda. Those eight documents included a Grab privacy notice and two Grab addenda. A particularly useful example came from Shopee: I could find the passage about service providers, but the wording left open whether a provider was acting for the company or in another capacity. The keyword hit helped me locate the passage. My review identified the question to ask next.

My feedback informed prompt revisions, and Edoardo reran the Shopee and NTU examples using the AI-assisted analysis. The project also includes a separate Grab comparison showing what V2’s contextual analysis added to V1’s keyword results. My eight-document test and that version comparison answered different questions.

Version 3 · What we want to improve

Versions 1 and 2 are complete. For Version 3, we’ve discussed structured outputs and batch analysis so a reviewer could compare several notices and revisit the same questions consistently. Those are planned improvements. The part I’d carry forward is the manual-review step: someone reading a flag should be able to understand why it appeared and what evidence would help resolve it.
