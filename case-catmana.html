I picked Xscape for CatMana because, well… the cat escaped. 🐥

Community research · Animal welfare · Singapore · On hold

CatMana · Lost cat? Find lah.

How it started

I’ve wanted to do something to help lost cats find their way home for a while. I have a silver tabby called Grey, so it’s a subject close to my heart. The final push came in September 2026, when my mum sent me a WhatsApp message about a cream-coloured Persian someone had spotted in Tampines. The cat looked frightened and had no collar. From the message alone, we couldn’t tell whether someone was looking for it or what had happened since the sighting.

My mum wondered whether, if the cat was microchipped, there was a way for the person who found it to get a message to its owner. I kept putting myself in the finder’s shoes. Say I’m the one standing there with the cat. I have a photo, a location and a genuine desire to help. Who do I contact first? Would a neighbourhood caregiver recognise it? If the owner posted about it that morning, would I even know where to look?

The next day, I started writing a concept note under the working name PawBack SG. My mum gave me the question that set things in motion. I took it from there as CatMana’s founder and project lead. Grey himself went missing the following month and thankfully came home. By then, I’d already spent weeks thinking about these questions from the finder’s side. This time I was the owner waiting for news.

Why I called it CatMana

I wanted a little Singlish in the name because this is a project I thought of here, for a situation I could picture happening in any Singapore neighbourhood. I also wanted the name to do some work. If someone saw it on a missing-cat notice, ideally they could make a decent guess at what it meant before reading the smaller print.

I played around with KetMana and ManaCat. What I liked about CatMana was that you could read it exactly as it was written. “Cat” is English, “mana” means “where” in Malay, and together you can hear the question: cat mana? It’s short, it makes sense, and it sounds like something I might actually type in the family chat where the idea began.

Then came the tagline, “Lost cat? Find lah.” The name sounds like the owner asking a question. The tagline sounds like a neighbour who’s already getting up to help look. That’s the bit I’m especially fond of.

What I researched

I started by tracing what could happen to that first WhatsApp message. An owner might be searching one lost-pet group while a finder shares a photo in a different neighbourhood chat. Meanwhile, the person best placed to recognise the cat might be the caregiver who feeds it every evening. All three people can have useful information and still have no obvious way to find one another.

I mapped the routes already available in Singapore, including the Animal & Veterinary Service’s pet licensing system, the SPCA, the Cat Welfare Society, community caregivers and online lost-pet channels. I looked at where veterinary clinics fit into the identification process and how an update might travel back to the person who first reported the sighting. Three questions kept shaping the research:

  • Does this cat need help getting home? A cat outdoors could be a missing pet or a familiar community cat going about its day. Someone who sees it regularly may know the difference. I wanted to account for that local knowledge before designing anything that might encourage a well-meaning finder to move it.
  • What does a microchip actually solve? My mum’s question made me look closely at the steps involved. Someone has to bring the cat to a place with an appropriate scanner, the chip number has to be checked against a registry, and someone then has to reach the registered owner. I became interested in who handles each step and what the finder can reasonably do while waiting.
  • Who needs to see which details? A general area can help neighbours recognise a sighting. The exact spot where a frightened cat is hiding may be better shared directly with the person following up. I wanted to think through that distinction, alongside how to let people know when a cat is home so an old post doesn’t keep sending neighbours out to search.

What I’ve developed so far

My work so far includes the original concept note, desk research into Singapore’s lost-and-found processes, and a map of how information moves between owners, finders, caregivers, vets and welfare organisations. That research gave me a basis for exploring nearby sighting alerts, private follow-up and case-status updates. Each idea has a specific job. A status update, for instance, could answer the question anyone who has ever forwarded a missing-cat post eventually asks: did they find the cat?

I also explored CatMana’s visual identity through moodboards, colour studies and a cat-shaped location-pin icon. I wanted the icon to make sense even at the size of a phone notification. The cat tells you who you’re looking for, and the pin tells you why where matters.

One decision I felt quite strongly about from the beginning was cost. I’d feel uncomfortable asking someone to pay at the point when they’re frightened about a missing pet. That instinct shaped the project early on and is part of why I think of CatMana as a community initiative.

Where it stands

CatMana is on hold at the research and concept stage. When I return to it, I’d like to speak with owners, finders, caregivers and veterinary professionals about searches they’ve actually been involved in. I’d ask each person to walk me through one case. Where did the first report go? Which update helped? At what point did they lose track of what happened next?

Their answers would help me decide what’s worth building. The outcome I care about is quite simple: if someone spots a cat in Tampines and someone else is at home worrying about it, I’d like those two people to have a better chance of finding each other. 🐥
